Literature attention retrospectively predicts clinical target selection in systemic lupus erythematosus, but largely as a generic immune-druggability prior

[Author list, affiliations, corresponding author]
Correspondence: [email]

Motivation. Gene-level attention in the biomedical literature is often used, implicitly or explicitly, to prioritise drug targets. Whether it actually anticipates which targets enter clinical development for a given disease has rarely been tested, and almost never against the possibility that any such signal is disease-agnostic.

Results. We built a literature-derived gene ranking for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) from 114,172 PubMed articles and machine-read gene annotations, then truncated the corpus at each year from 2010 to 2018 and asked whether the resulting ranking anticipated the targets that subsequently entered SLE clinical trials, dated from ClinicalTrials.gov. The ranking separated future clinical targets from the remaining gene universe at a median AUC of 0.799 (range 0.768–0.936; p ≤ 0.017 at every cutoff). Recency of attention outperformed cumulative attention (AUC 0.863 vs 0.801 at the 2015 cutoff). However, the same lupus-derived ranking predicted target druggability in 26 other immune-mediated diseases at a median AUC of 0.712 — 0.729 for rheumatoid arthritis and 0.742 for psoriasis — indicating that most of the apparent predictive signal is a generic immune-druggability prior, with a disease-specific increment of roughly 0.10 AUC.

Availability. [Zenodo DOI]; dashboard at [URL]; analysis code at [repository].

1 · Introduction

Target selection in immune-mediated disease draws on genetics, tractability, and precedent, but also on a softer signal: how much the literature is currently talking about a gene. Attention is cheap to measure and is embedded in most public target-prioritisation resources, whether as an explicit text-mining evidence class or implicitly through curation that follows published work.

The obvious criticism is circularity — genes accumulate papers because a programme is underway, so attention may lag discovery rather than lead it. The less obvious criticism, and the one we test here, is that attention may carry real predictive signal that is nonetheless not specific to the disease in question: well-studied immune genes get drugged in immune diseases generally, and a lupus-derived ranking might do just as well at anticipating rheumatoid arthritis programmes.

We address both by time-travelling a lupus literature ranking to nine historical cutoffs and evaluating it against subsequently-registered SLE trials, then repeating the evaluation across 26 comparator indications.

2 · Methods

2.1 Corpus and gene ranking

The corpus is every PubMed record matching "lupus erythematosus, systemic"[MeSH] OR lupus[tiab] (114,172 articles). Gene mentions in titles and abstracts were taken from PubTator 3 and normalised to Entrez identifiers; mouse and rat homologs were merged into the corresponding human gene by symbol, and non-human-only annotations dropped. Genes mentioned in fewer than five articles were excluded, leaving 1,505 ranked genes.

Each gene receives a score from cumulative attention and recent attention, both log-scaled and max-normalised across the ranked set:

score = 100 × ( 4⁄7 · log1p(papers)/max + 3⁄7 · log1p(paperslast 5y)/max )

This is the literature component of the deployed dashboard score, renormalised with the third component — a curated Open Targets association score — removed, because that component cannot be reconstructed at historical cutoffs from current data.

Scope decision outstanding. The deployed tool also computes a 16-criterion target-opportunity score spanning tractability, safety and competitive whitespace. None of those 12 additional criteria are validated here. The paper must either extend the evaluation to them (requiring archived Open Targets releases) or state explicitly in the title and abstract that only the literature ranking is under test.

2.2 Retrospective evaluation

For each cutoff year c from 2010 to 2018, the corpus was truncated to articles published in or before c and the ranking recomputed from scratch, including the five-paper inclusion threshold and the normalisation constants. The evaluation universe is the genes rankable at c, excluding those already in SLE clinical development by c — a contemporaneous observer already knew about those, so retaining them would score the model on cases where it could not have been wrong.

Positives are genes whose first registered SLE trial post-dates c. Entry dates are the earliest ClinicalTrials.gov first-posted date across all trials with a lupus condition and that gene's drugs as an intervention, using 81 international nonproprietary names plus 58 development codes; querying INNs alone dates programmes years late, since early-phase trials are typically registered under a development code.

Two rules still unwritten. (i) Multi-target attribution: TYK2 dates to 2015 via tofacitinib, a pan-JAK inhibitor, or to 2019 via the selective agent deucravacitinib. Both are defensible and they give different answers. (ii) Synonym completeness: development codes were curated by hand and are certainly incomplete, biasing some entry dates late. A programmatic source (ChEMBL) should replace the hand list.

2.3 Cross-indication control

To test disease specificity, the 2015 lupus ranking was evaluated against target sets for 26 other immune-mediated indications, with labels drawn from Open Targets drug records under identical filtering. Drugs acting on more than five gene products (proteasome, tubulin and polymerase subunits) and agents used in SLE for supportive care or comorbidity rather than as mechanism-driven programmes were excluded throughout.

Not yet date-matched. The SLE arm is time-travelled; the comparator labels are current. Until two or three comparators receive the same truncation, the 0.10 AUC increment is not a clean estimate of disease specificity. This is the single most important outstanding analysis.

2.4 Statistics

Discrimination is reported as the area under the ROC curve, equivalent here to the probability that a randomly chosen future clinical target outranks a randomly chosen non-target. Significance is by permutation on the median percentile of positives (10,000–20,000 draws). Genes sharing a clinical programme — the IL-12/23 subunits under ustekinumab, the JAK family under pan-JAK inhibitors, CD19 and FCGR2B under obexelimab — are collapsed to their best-ranked member so that co-targeted genes are not counted as independent successes.

Missing. Bootstrap confidence intervals (essential — later cutoffs rest on three to five positives) and a stated multiplicity position across nine cutoffs and four score variants.

3 · Results

3.1 Attention anticipates clinical entry

At the 2015 cutoff the corpus comprises 72,997 articles and 889 rankable genes. Excluding the 16 genes already in SLE clinical development leaves a universe of 858. The ranking separated subsequent clinical entrants from the remainder at AUC 0.826, with positives sitting at a median percentile of 14.6% against an expected 50% (p = 0.0022).

3.2 The signal is stable across cutoffs

Re-running the evaluation at every year from 2010 to 2018 gives a median AUC of 0.799 across nine independent cutoffs, with the universe growing from 656 to 1,003 genes and no degradation in discrimination.

3.3 Recency outperforms cumulative attention

Ablating the score into its components at the 2015 cutoff shows that recent attention alone discriminates better than the weighted combination, and that the five-year-over-five-year velocity ratio — the component that reads most intuitively as "momentum" — carries little independent signal.

3.4 Hand-assigned dates inflate the estimate

An initial analysis using entry years assigned from domain knowledge rather than the registry gave a median AUC of 0.875 (range 0.849–0.932). Replacing those with ClinicalTrials.gov first-posted dates lowered it to 0.799. The discrepancy was systematic rather than random: five programmes proved to have entered clinical development substantially earlier than assumed — ICOS in 2008 rather than 2015, CLEC4C in 2014 rather than 2016, S1PR1 in 2014 rather than 2017 — moving them out of the positive set and into the excluded already-in-clinic set. We report the registry-dated result throughout and note the difference as a caution to retrospective analyses that rely on expert recall for labels.

3.5 Most of the signal is not lupus-specific

Applying the 2015 lupus ranking to 26 other immune-mediated indications produced a median AUC of 0.712. SLE ranked second of 27 at 0.810, but rheumatoid arthritis reached 0.729 and psoriatic arthritis 0.790 — on a ranking derived entirely from the lupus literature.

4 · Discussion

Literature attention does anticipate clinical target selection in lupus, consistently and significantly, across nine independent historical cutoffs. That is a real result and it is not explained by the most obvious confound, since genes already in clinical development at each cutoff are excluded from the universe by construction.

But the cross-indication control substantially qualifies what that result means. A ranking built entirely from the lupus literature predicts rheumatoid arthritis and psoriasis target sets nearly as well as it predicts lupus. Most of what the ranking captures is therefore not lupus biology but a general property of the immune target landscape: a comparatively small set of well-studied immune genes attracts both papers and drug programmes, across diseases. The lupus-specific increment is real but modest.

This has a direct practical implication. If attention is largely a generic prior, then the value of a disease-specific prioritisation tool must come from its other components — genetic evidence, tractability, safety, and competitive whitespace — rather than from the literature ranking that is easiest to compute and most visible to users.

Limitations

  • The endpoint is clinical entry, not clinical success. IL17A ranks among the strongest positives at nearly every cutoff, yet IL-17 blockade has not succeeded in SLE. A score that predicts what is attempted is measuring something different from one that predicts what works. Primary endpoint not yet chosen
  • A consistent blind spot. CLEC4C ranked 359th of 889 in 2015; litifilimab, which targets it, is among the more notable recent SLE programmes. Attention-based ranking would not have surfaced it at any cutoff tested.
  • Registry censoring. ClinicalTrials.gov opened in 2000 and became broadly mandatory around 2005, so entry years before that are censored. This affects the excluded already-in-clinic set rather than the positives for cutoffs from 2010 onward, but the bound has not been quantified.
  • Machine-read annotation error. Gene mentions come from PubTator without manual verification; no false-positive rate has been estimated. ~200-pair adjudication pending
  • Comparator baseline. Discrimination is reported against random ranking only. The relevant comparison — against Open Targets' own target-prioritisation ordering on the same holdout — has not been run. Blocking
  • Weight sensitivity. The 4⁄7 ∕ 3⁄7 weighting is inherited from the deployed dashboard and is demonstrably not optimal, since recency alone scores higher. No sensitivity analysis over the weight simplex has been performed. Pending
